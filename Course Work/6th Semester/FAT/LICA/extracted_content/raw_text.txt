Unit 5: Introduction Filters
A filter is a circuit that allows desired frequencies to pass and attenuates unwanted frequencies.
Filters are of two types: analog and digital; analog filters are further classified as passive and active.
Passive filters use R, L, C components, while active filters use op-amps with resistors and capacitors, reducing loading effects.
Filters are widely used in communication and signal processing for noise reduction, interference suppression, bandwidth control, and signal shaping.

Unit 5: Limitations of Passive Filters
In RLC networks, the practical inductors tend to deviate from the inductance realised.
There is a power loss because of the dissipation in resistive and inductive elements of the passive network.
For most practical inductors, the total dissipative losses tend to increase with frequency, since Q does not increase linearly with frequency.
At low frequency (less than 1 kHz) applications such as control systems and analog computers, practical inductors of reasonable Q tend to become bulky and expensive.
The magnetic coupling creates additional problem in the form of unwanted and stray field which is harmful for applications such as satellite instrumentation.
The dissipative losses start increasing when an inductor is miniaturised. The Q of a lowfrequency inductor is roughly proportional to the square of the scaling factor.

Unit 5: Advantages of Active Filters
The maximum value of the transfer function or gain may be greater than unity (passive<=1).
The loading effect is minimal, which means that the output response of the filter is essentially independent of the load driven by the filter.
The active filters do not exhibit insertion loss. Hence, the passband gain is equal to 0dB or higher.
Active filters replace inductors with op-amps + RC networks.
Inductors become impractical at low frequencies. Passive networks can only be practically realized for high frequencies. Active filters solve this.
The active filters are easily tunable due to flexibility in gain and frequency adjustments.
The op-amp has high input impedance and low output impedance. Hence, the active filters using op-amp do not cause loading effect on the source and load. Therefore, cascading of networks does not need buffer amplifier.
Active filters can be made tunable using control voltage or variable components.

Unit 5: Limitations of Active Filters
The high frequency response is limited by the gain-bandwidth product and slew rate of the practical op-amps, leading to comparatively lower bandwidth than the designed bandwidth.
The design of active filters becomes costly for high frequencies. Require high slew rate Op-amps.
Active filters require dual polarity dc power supply whereas passive filters do not. Passive filter works purely on input signal. Whereas, Op-amp needs external power supply to work.
The active element is prone to the process parameter variations and they are sensitive to ambient conditions like temperature. Hence, the performance of the active filter deviates from the ideal response.

Unit 5: Active Filter Design
The manner in which the behaviour of the filter varies with frequency is called its frequency response and it is expressed in terms of the transfer function H(jw)
Low Pass Filter: A low-pass filter allows only low frequency signals up to a certain break-point fH to pass through, while suppressing high frequency components. Frequencies from 0 to fH is called passband and else is called stopband
High Pass Filter: A high-pass filter allows only frequencies above a certain break-point fL to pass through and attenuates the low frequency components. Frequencies from 0 to fL is called stopband and else is called passband
Bandpass Filter: The bandpass filter is the combination of high and low-pass filters, and this allows a specified range of frequencies to pass through.
Band Reject Filter: The band-reject filter is the logical inverse of bandpass filter, which does not allow a specified range of frequencies to pass through.

Unit 5: First Order Band Pass Filters
A band-pass filter is formed by cascading a low-pass and a high-pass filter.
The low-pass section passes frequencies up to fH​, while the high-pass blocks below fL​
Proper operation requires fH>fL​, typically fH≥10*fL for a clear passband.
Both sections must have equal passband gain to ensure uniform response in the band fL​ to fH​.
A ±20 dB/decade band-pass filter is realized using first-order high-pass and low-pass sections with op-amps.
Higher roll-off (±40 dB/decade) is achieved by cascading second-order filter sections for sharper selectivity.

Unit 5: First Order Band Reject Filters
A band-reject (band-stop) filter is formed by connecting a low-pass and high-pass filter in parallel.
The inputs are applied in parallel, and outputs are combined using a summing circuit to prevent loading.​
The low-pass section blocks frequencies above fH, while the high-pass blocks below fL​.
Proper operation requires fL​>fH​, creating a stopband​.
The resulting output passes frequencies outside this range and rejects those within the stopband.
A wideband band-reject filter is realized by paralleling HPF (fL​) and LPF (fH​) with a summing amplifier.
The summing amplifier adds outputs of both sections to produce the final band-reject response.
Equal passband gain in both HPF and LPF sections is necessary for proper filter performance.
The frequency response shows attenuation in the band fH​ to fL​ and passbands on either side.

Unit 5: Chebyshev Filters
How the Denominator Controls the Response Shape:
Inside the Passband (ω ≤ ωc): C_N oscillates between −1 and +1, so C_N² is between 0 and 1. 
Denominator oscillates between sqrt(1+0) = 1 and sqrt(1+ε²). Response oscillates between A (maximum) and A/sqrt(1+ε²) (minimum). 
This bounded oscillation is the equiripple behaviour.
Inside the Stopband (ω > ωc): C_N grows without bound (exponentially fast) as ω increases.
Denominator becomes very large, driving |H| rapidly toward zero.
This explosive growth is what gives Chebyshev its sharp roll-off.
The larger N is, the faster C_N grows and the sharper the cut-off frequency roll off.

Unit 5: All Pass Filters
An all-pass filter allows all frequency components to pass with unity gain. It modifies only the phase response without affecting amplitude.
The primary function is to introduce a controlled phase shift across frequencies. This makes it useful for phase alignment and signal timing correction.
All-pass filters are also called delay equalisers, phase correctors, or constant delay filters.These names reflect their role in maintaining amplitude while altering phase.
Basic phase lag and phase lead circuits act as simple all-pass filters. This occurs when feedback and input resistances are equal (Rf = R1).
Unlike fixed-phase circuits (inverting amplifier, integrator, differentiator etc), all-pass filters provide adjustable phase shift.
They are widely used in communication systems for phase equalization. This ensures signal integrity without altering amplitude characteristics.
The transfer function typically has zeros that are mirror images of poles. This symmetry ensures constant magnitude response across all frequencies.

Unit 5: State Variable Filters
State-variable filter (SVF) is a versatile second-order active filter. From one circuit, you can obtain high-pass, band-pass, low-pass, and notch outputs.
It is also called a universal filter because the same topology gives multiple responses. The circuit uses two integrators and one summing amplifier.
The resistors R and capacitors C are the timing elements. They mainly set the natural frequency of the filter.
The feedback network controls the damping factor 𝑎. Equivalently, it controls Q, so tuning and selectivity can be adjusted separately.
A major advantage is electronic tuning over a wide frequency range. You can change frequency without destroying the filter structure.
Another advantage is independent adjustment of 𝑄 and 𝜔0​. That is a big practical advantage over many other second-order filters.
The band-pass output is taken from the first integrator stage.
The low-pass output is obtained after the second integrator.
The notch output is formed by summing the high-pass and low-pass signals.

Unit 5: Switched Capacitor Filters
Switched capacitor (SC) filters are discrete-time filters that replace resistors using capacitors and switches.
Earlier continuous-time filters relied on R, C, and op-amps. Their performance depended on absolute values of resistors and capacitors, which are inaccurate in CMOS.
In CMOS technology, resistors occupy large chip area and have poor tolerance. Capacitors, however, have much better matching accuracy, making them preferable.
The fundamental idea is that a switched capacitor behaves like a resistor.This equivalent resistance depends on clock frequency and capacitance.
When a capacitor is switched between two nodes, it transfers charge periodically. This creates an average current equivalent to resistive current flow.
This is the key principle of SC filters: resistors are replaced by capacitors + MOS switches + clock signals.

To summarise the features of SC integrator,
 (i) there are no resistors involved as necessitated for IC fabrication.
 (ii) the unity gain frequency fo is determined by capacitance ratios, which are easier to control and maintain in present-day IC technology.
 (iii) the unity gain frequency fo is proportional to the clock frequency fck , thus proving that the SC filters are of programmable type.
The practical limitations of SC filters are as follows:
 (i) The values of fck are not unlimited, which is controlled by the quality of MOS switches, and the speed of op-amp employed. Practical values of fck are in MHz range.
 (ii) The lower limit of fck is dictated by the leakage currents in MOS switches and the input bias currents of op-amp. Hence, the minimum possible fck range is typically 100 Hz.
 (iii) The SC filters are discrete-time operated.

Unit 6: Specifications of DAC
Linearity: The most common dynamic errors are full-scale error and linearity error. Full-scale error is the maximum deviation of the output value from its expected or ideal value, expressed in percentage of full-scale. Linearity error is the maximum deviation in step size from the ideal step size.
Linearity means how evenly the analog output increases for each equal step in digital input (ideal DAC gives perfectly equal steps).
In real DACs, resistor imperfections cause gain and offset errors, so the output deviates from a straight line (non-linear).

Unit 6: Specifications of DAC
Resolution: Resolution is the smallest change in output corresponding to a 1-bit change (1 LSB).


Settling Time: The time required for the output of a D/A converter to settle down to within ±(1/2) LSB of the final value for a given digital input is known as settling time. It depends on the switching time of the logic circuits, which in turn depends on the inevitable stray capacitances and inductances present in the converter circuit.
Temperature sensitivity: Temperature sensitivity means the analog output changes slightly with temperature even if the digital input is fixed, due to components like reference voltage, resistors, and op-amp.

Unit 6: DAC
A DAC converts an n-bit binary number into a proportional analog voltage or current using a reference voltage. 
The binary input D is made of bits b1	​ (MSB) to bn	​ (LSB), each having decreasing weights 2−1,2−2,…,2−n.

Each bit carries either 0 or 1. Adding together you can get binary fractional number.
For example: 101=1⋅2−1+0⋅2−2+1⋅2−3=(1/2)+0+(1/8)​=0.625

Unit 6: Weighted resistor DAC
A binary-weighted resistor DAC is one of the simplest ways to convert digital data into an analog signal.
It converts each bit of a digital input into a proportional analog contribution, and then adds all contributions together to form the final output.
Instead of doing digital multiplication, the circuit uses resistors since I=V/R
The circuit assigns different resistor values to different bits so that each bit creates a different current contribution.
LSB gets the largest resistor. Each more significant bit gets half the resistor. therefore, each more significant bit contributes twice the effect of the next lower bit.

Unit 6: Weihted resistor DAC
Each bit controls a switch. The switch has two positions: If the bit is 1, it connects to the reference voltage −VR else it connects to ground. 
So each bit either contributes a weighted current from the reference, or no contribution at all.
The Op-amp is used as the summing amplifier. The currents are all added at the inverting terminal of Op-amp.

Unit 6: Drawbacks of Weighted resistor DAC
Binary weighted DAC needs a wide range of resistor values; as bit count increases, resistor ratios become very large (e.g., 128:1 for 8-bit).
Such large resistors are hard to fabricate accurately and cause errors due to bias currents, reducing accuracy.
Because of this, weighted DACs are usually limited to low resolutions (below 8-bit).
R-2R ladder DAC avoids this by using only two resistor values, making it more practical and accurate.

Simpler to fabricate as the range of resistances is tremendously reduced for higher order bits.
