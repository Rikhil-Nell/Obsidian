5.1 Introduction
Over the years, significant progress has been made in reducing the power consumption of Random-Access Memory (RAM).
 Numerous circuit-level techniques have been developed to minimize both active and standby power in static as well as dynamic memory architectures.
 This advancement has been driven by the rapid growth of compact, energy-efficient electronic devices such as mobile phones, laptops, and portable systems.
 RAM is a read/write memory that allows data stored in its cells to be modified quickly and efficiently.
 A key feature of RAM is that the time required for read or write operations remains constant, irrespective of the physical location of the data within the memory.

5.2 Basics of SRAM
An SRAM chip consists of several important components, including address buffers, row decoders, column decoders, memory array, sense amplifiers, input/output buffers, and precharge circuits, all of which work together to perform read and write operations efficiently.
The memory array is the core part of the SRAM, where data bits are stored in individual memory cells, and each cell is connected to a specific word line and a pair of complementary bit lines (BL and BL̅).
The process of cell selection begins when the address is applied to the address buffers, which then drive the row and column decoders to activate a particular word line and bit line corresponding to the given address.
The selected memory cell is determined by the intersection of the activated word line and bit line, which uniquely identifies the location of the data within the memory array.
Dr. Aruru Sai Kumar , VIT-AP

Basics of SRAM
During a read operation, the selected word line is enabled, allowing the stored data in the cell to create a small voltage difference on the bit lines, which is then detected and amplified by the sense amplifier to produce a full logic-level output.
During a write operation, the input data is applied to the bit lines, and when the word line is activated, the data is forced into the selected memory cell, thereby updating its stored value.
The access time of SRAM is defined as the time required for the data to propagate from the address input to the data output, and it is mainly influenced by delays in word-line decoding and bit-line sensing.

To minimize access time and improve performance, SRAM design typically uses small voltage swings on bit lines and highly sensitive sense amplifiers to enable faster detection of stored data. 
SRAM operation is controlled by signals such as Write Enable (WE), Chip Select (CS), and Output Enable (OE), where WE determines whether the operation is read or write, CS selects a specific memory chip from a group of chips, and OE enables the output buffer during read operations.

Precharge and Equalization Circuit:
Purpose of Bit-Line Precharge
Bit-line precharge is used in SRAM circuits to initialize the bit-lines (BL and BL to a known voltage level before every read operation, ensuring accurate and fast sensing).
     Equalization Mechanism (Φ_eq)
The equalization signal (Φ_eq) activates a transistor that connects BL and BL, forcing both lines to the same potential and eliminating any voltage difference before read.
	nMOS Precharge Design (Figure a)
In this configuration, nMOS transistors are used as load devices to precharge the bit-lines, typically pulling them toward a lower voltage level relative to VDD.
Limitations of nMOS Loads
nMOS-based precharge circuits suffer from threshold voltage (VT) drop, which prevents full voltage swing (cannot reach exact VDD), leading to reduced noise margin.
Dr. Aruru Sai Kumar , VIT-AP

Precharge and Equalization Circuit:
pMOS Precharge Design (Figure b)
pMOS transistors are used to precharge bit-lines directly to VDD, providing a full voltage swing and improving reliability during sensing.
Advantage of pMOS Precharge
Since pMOS does not suffer from threshold drop when pulling high, it ensures that BL and BL are fully charged to VDD, enhancing read stability.
Role of Word Line (WL)
The word line controls access to the memory cell. During precharge, WL is kept low to isolate the memory cell from the bit-lines.
Interaction with Memory Cell
Once precharge is complete, WL is activated, and the stored data in the memory cell causes a slight voltage difference between BL and BL for sensing.

5.2.1 Low-Power SRAM Technologies
Sources of SRAM power: 
The power present in an SRAM is categorized into two parts , active and standby (data retention) power.
 The active power is the sum of the power consumed by the row and column decoders, memory array, sense amplifiers, and peripheral circuits such as the input/output buffers. 
The effective data retention current of the unselected memory cells when the sense amplifiers are significantly disabled contributes to the standby power of an SRAM. 
Static current from other sources is negligible.
Dr. Aruru Sai Kumar , VIT-AP

[LPVLSI_MODULE-5.pptx] Page/Slide 32

Low Power SRAM Circuits
SRAM chip development has been driven by low power circuit applications, although the emphasis has recently been on more enhanced speed.
Partial activation of a multi-divided word line and pulse operation of word-line circuitry are typical examples of low power circuits, which drastically reduce the DC current that dominates total active current.
Pulse operation of the column/sense circuitry is another example of low power techniques. The pulse operation is essential to reducing IDCP.
ATD (Address Transition Detection) plays an important role in the pulse operations for word-line and column/sense circuitry.

Partial Activation of Multi-divided Word Lines:

The multiple row decoder scheme and double-word line scheme, which divides a word line into sub word lines, greatly reduces the static current of SRAMs. A more sophisticated word-line division, called DWL (Divided Word Line), adopts a two-stage hierarchical row decoder structure, as shown in Figure.
The DWL scheme requires two levels of metal layers; one for a main word line and the other for a data line. The number of sub-word lines connected to one main word line in the data-line direction is generally four (at most eight), compromising an area to a main row decoder with an area to a local row decoder.

5.3 Basics of DRAM
Each DRAM memory cell consists of a single pair of nMOS transistor and capacitor. It is also commonly known as the one-transistor (1T) cell, which renders smaller chip size and lower cost.
 It can perform both read and write operations.
The word-line (WL) is the output signal from row decoder and controls the transistor gate running perpendicular to the Bit-Line (BL). In contrast to the SRAM cell where two BLs are used, DRAM utilizes only one BL. 
Reading and writing is accomplished by turning on the access transistor with the word-line.

Basics of DRAM
The storage capacitor Cs is used to store the data in the form of charge, whereas Cp, denotes the capacitance at the BL, including the parasitic load of the connected circuits. 
The role of Cs to accumulate charge necessitates periodical refreshing of the cell to avoid information loss.

Basics of DRAM
Read and Write Operation:
Prior to the read operation, the bit-line parasitic capacitance (CBL) is precharged to logic half-VDD, where VDD is the power supply voltage.
Subsequently, WL is pulled high in order to activate the nMOS-type access transistor. 
Read “1” Operation.
 When WL is asserted, the voltage at BL tends to increase slightly if the charge stored in Cs is an ideal logic “1.” 
This implies that a logic “1” is stored in the memory cell. However,  the charge stored in Cs tends to leak off gradually. Therefore its charge is usually not at ideal logic “1.”

============================================================
[LPVLSI_MODULE-5.pptx] Page/Slide 42
============================================================

Basics of DRAM
Read “0” Operation. 
The charge is shared by CBL and CS. As CBL discharges and CS charges up, the voltage at BL will decrease slightly, indicating that a logic “0” is being stored in the capacitor. 
As for the write case, the data to be written is imprinted on the data I/O lines and then applied to the selected bit-line by using the column decoder. 
When a particular memory cell is chosen, the data is written on the BL by forcing a high or a low value.

Basics of DRAM
Write “1” Operation. 
The bit-line potential is pulled to logic “1” by charging CBL to either VDD or (VDD -Vth) depending on the type of write circuitry used, while the selected word-line is pulled high by the row address decoder. 
Vth, implies the threshold voltage of the nMOS transistor. 
The access transistor will then turn on by asserting the WL, allowing the storage capacitor to be charged up to a logic “1” level.
Write “0” Operation. 
Bit-line is pulled to logic “0” by the write circuitry whereas the selected word-line is raised by the row address decoder.
 The access transistor turns on and storage capacitor discharges if it is initially storing a logic “1” or remains unchanged if it is already storing a logic “0.”

5.4.1 Self Refresh Circuit
In DRAM, data is stored as charge in capacitors. 
Due to leakage currents, this stored charge gradually decays with time. 
If the voltage drops below a threshold, the stored data (0 or 1) becomes invalid. 
 Therefore, refreshing is mandatory to restore the original charge before it degrades.

Self Refresh Circuit
DRAM stores data as charge in capacitors, which leaks over time, so periodic refreshing is essential.
 In self-refresh mode, the DRAM refreshes itself without external control using on-chip circuitry.
Step-by-Step Operation:
Entering Self-Refresh Mode
Triggered by a control signal (e.g., clock disable in DDR).
External clock and memory controller activity are halted.
Internal Clock Generation
A low-power internal oscillator generates periodic timing pulses.

SDRAM (Synchronous DRAM)
SDRAM is a type of DRAM that operates synchronously with the system clock, improving speed over conventional DRAM. 
It transfers one data word per clock cycle (single data rate). 
It introduced features like burst access and pipelining, making it widely used in early computers. 
 DDR SDRAM (DDR1)
DDR SDRAM improves SDRAM by transferring data on both rising and falling edges of the clock, effectively doubling data rate. 
This provides higher bandwidth without increasing clock frequency. 
It also operates at lower voltage and better efficiency than SDRAM.

5.5 Basics of ROM
The fundamental architecture of a Read-Only Memory (ROM) consists of a memory cell array organized in a matrix form, along with row and column address decoders that are used to uniquely select one word from a total of 2n stored words based on an n-bit input address.
The row decoder activates one specific word-line corresponding to the given address, while the column decoder selects the required bit-lines to access the desired data from the memory array efficiently.
A conditioning circuit is employed to precharge the bit-lines prior to the read operation, thereby improving the speed and reliability of data sensing.
The stored information is retrieved from the selected memory cells through bit-lines and is subsequently detected using sense amplifiers, which convert small voltage differences into valid digital logic levels.

In many ROM designs, column decoders are utilized before the sensing stage to select a subset of bit-lines, reducing hardware complexity and improving overall performance.
Once the data is ready, the output enable signal activates the output buffers, allowing the retrieved data to be transferred to the external output terminals.
The chip select and output enable signals play a crucial role in memory expansion by enabling multiple memory chips to be combined, thereby increasing both the total number of words and the word size.

Basics of ROM
1 Kbit ROM Organization:
A typical 1 Kbit ROM can be organized as 256 words with 4 bits per word, requiring an 8-bit address input to uniquely identify each word.
The 8-bit address is divided into a 5-bit row address and a 3-bit column address, resulting in 32 word-lines and a memory cell array structured as a 32 × 32 matrix.
The row decoder selects one of the 32 rows, while four 8-to-1 column decoders act as data selectors to choose four specific columns corresponding to the required 4-bit output.
The selected data is then passed through output buffers, which are enabled by the chip select signal, to produce the final 4-bit output word (D0–D3).

Low-Power ROM Technology
Sources of power dissipation :
The conventional architecture of a ROM consists of a memory core, also known as the memory bank, which is formed by an array of transistors arranged systematically to store binary information.
Two primary types of ROM array organizations are the NAND array and the NOR array, where the pull-down transistors are connected in series in NAND structures and in parallel in NOR structures.
Among these configurations, the NOR-based ROM is more widely used due to its higher access speed compared to the NAND-based ROM architecture.
The address decoder plays a critical role by selecting one specific word-line in the ROM core at a time based on the applied input address.

Power Dissipation in ROM:
In a ROM system, a significant portion of power dissipation arises from highly capacitive components such as predecoder lines, word-lines, and bit-lines.
A typical implementation example is a 2K × 18 ROM designed using 0.6 µm technology, operating at a supply voltage of 3.3 V and a clock frequency of 10 MHz.
Among all sources of power consumption, the precharging of bit-lines in the ROM core contributes the largest share of total power dissipation.

Low-Power ROM Technology
Low-power techniques at architecture level:

Divided Hierarchical Word-Line Structure:
The divided hierarchical word-line technique partitions the memory core into smaller subblocks to reduce power consumption.
Global and local word-lines are implemented in different layers to enable efficient memory access.
Only the selected subblock is activated during a read operation, reducing unnecessary switching activity.
This approach can be applied to both SRAM and ROM architectures for power optimization.
However, due to layout constraints, a minimum multiplexer ratio (e.g., 4:1) is still required.
As a result, multiple bit-lines may still switch, leading to some remaining power loss.

Low-Power ROM Technology
Low-power techniques at architecture level:
ii) Selective Precharge:
In conventional operation, all bit-lines are precharged to a high voltage at the beginning of each cycle.
This results in large capacitance switching, as many bit-lines discharge even when they are not accessed.
Consequently, unnecessary switching leads to excessive dynamic power dissipation.
The selective precharge technique overcomes this by precharging only the bit-lines that are required for the read operation.
This significantly reduces switching activity and improves power efficiency.
The method is cost-effective since it uses existing control logic with minimal additional hardware overhead.

Low-power techniques at architecture level:

iii) Difference encoding:
In applications such as digital filters, ROM data is accessed sequentially and adjacent values often change gradually.
Instead of storing complete data values, only the difference between consecutive data values is stored, reducing memory size.
This technique, known as difference encoding, helps minimize the overall ROM core area.
The original data must be reconstructed using an adder, which introduces additional hardware complexity.
A variation of this method stores differences relative to fixed constants (offsets) to further optimize storage.

Low-Power ROM Technology
Low-power techniques at circuit level :
i) nMOS precharge:
 Voltage swing reduction is an effective technique to minimize power dissipation in ROM bit-lines.
Using nMOS transistors for precharging limits the bit-line voltage to VDD – VT, where VT is the threshold voltage.
As a result, bit-lines switch between VDD – VT   and ground, reducing voltage swing and dynamic power consumption.
This leads to significant power savings due to lower capacitive switching.
However, the technique degrades noise margin and is affected by body bias, which increases threshold voltage.
Therefore, careful design of output drivers is required to ensure reliable operation.

Low-power techniques at circuit level :
ii) Voltage scaling:
Voltage scaling is one of the most effective and widely used techniques for reducing power dissipation in digital circuits.
 A quadratic reduction in dynamic power can be achieved by lowering the supply voltage. 
Additionally, short-circuit power dissipation is significantly reduced as the supply voltage decreases, and several high-precision models have been proposed to estimate short-circuit currents . 
However, these power savings come at the cost of degraded circuit performance, as reducing the supply voltage leads to increased propagation delay and reduced operating speed.

5.6 Future Trend and Development of ROMs and DRAMs
EPROM, EEPROM, and Flash memory differ in features that influence their commercial use.
 UV-EPROM gained significant market share due to its high-density and field alterability, despite higher cost and limited flexibility. EEPROM is preferred in low-density applications (below 256 K) because of its high flexibility. 
Flash memory is increasingly popular due to its high density, low cost, and strong system flexibility, making it dominant in modern memory markets.

Future Trend and Development of ROMs and DRAMs
With increasing circuit density and functionality, advanced memory technologies are evolving. 
The Flash+ concept integrates Flash memory and EEPROM on a single chip to combine their advantages. 
Flash memory is ideal for storing microcontroller code due to its ability for dynamic updates, while EEPROM is preferred for storing parametric data because of its byte-level modification capability. 
Thus, Flash+ offers both flexibility and efficient data handling.
Earlier, DRAM dominated the memory market, but flash memory has now become the primary focus due to its lowest cost per bit, smaller cell size, and simpler manufacturing process.

Future Trend and Development of ROMs and DRAMs
The rapid growth of wireless consumer devices , such as smartphones, digital cameras, and handheld electronics has significantly driven the demand and advancement of flash memory technology.
To increase flash memory density, vendors are developing techniques to store multiple bits in a single memory cell.
 Traditionally, memories such as DRAM, EPROM, EEPROM, and earlier flash devices store only one bit per cell, typically using a capacitor-based structure.
In conventional memory cells, the stored charge determines whether the bit is 0 or 1.
 Modern flash memory uses multiple charge levels within a single cell, enabling storage of two or more bits per cell. This increases storage density and further reduces the cost per bit, making flash memory more efficient and attractive.
