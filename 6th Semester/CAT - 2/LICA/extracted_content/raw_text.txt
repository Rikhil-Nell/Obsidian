Summing amplifier
A summing amplifier (adder) is an inverting op-amp configuration whose output is the algebraic sum of multiple input signals.
If all input resistors are equal (𝑅1=𝑅2=⋯=𝑅𝑛), the circuit becomes an equal-weighted summing amplifier.
Each input has an independent gain determined by its input resistor, with minimal interaction between inputs.
Common applications include audio mixing, signal combining, and level shifting (adding a DC offset to an AC signal).

Subtractor amplifier
A subtractor (difference amplifier) is realized using an op-amp with four equal resistors 𝑅1=𝑅2=𝑅3=𝑅𝑓.
Using the superposition principle, the output due to 𝑉2​ alone acts as a unity-gain non-inverting amplifier, while the output due to 𝑉1​ alone acts as a unity-gain inverting amplifier.
Hence, the circuit produces an output proportional to the difference between the two input voltages and functions as a unity-gain difference (subtractor) amplifier.

Integrator
An op-amp integrator is obtained by replacing the feedback resistor of an inverting amplifier with a capacitor 𝐶𝑓.
Due to virtual ground at the inverting input and negligible input bias current, the output voltage is proportional to the negative time integral of the input voltage.
At DC (𝜔=0), the gain becomes theoretically infinite, causing output saturation in practical circuits, while higher frequencies produce stable integration behavior.

Limitations of Ideal integrator:
In an ideal op-amp integrator, even in the absence of an input signal, input offset voltage and bias current produce an error voltage at the output, causing distortion and preventing true integration.
To limit low-frequency gain and avoid saturation, a resistor is connected in parallel with the integrating capacitor, but this also restricts the high-frequency integration range, making the circuit non-ideal.

Differentiator
An op-amp differentiator produces an output voltage proportional to the rate of change of the input voltage, making it useful for detecting rapid signal variations.
The ideal differentiator is obtained by replacing the input resistor of an inverting amplifier with a capacitor, effectively interchanging the resistor and capacitor positions of an integrator.
A differentiator performs the inverse operation of an integrator and emphasizes high-frequency components, with the upper cut-off frequency determined by the RC time constant.

Op-Amp Internal Circuit
An op-amp is a multistage, direct-coupled, high-gain amplifier that uses negative feedback, consists of one or more differential amplifier stages, and ends with a level translator and an output stage.
The input (differential) stage, often implemented using two cascaded differential amplifiers, provides high input resistance and the initial voltage gain of the op-amp.
Differential amplifier is a basic building block of an op-amp. The function of a differential amplifier is to amplify the difference between two input signals. The two transistors Q1 and Q2 have identical characteristics.
The resistances of the circuits are equal, i.e. RE1 = R E2, RC1 = R C2 and the magnitude of +VCC is equal to the magnitude of -VEE. These voltages are measured with respect to ground.

Level Shifter
Direct coupling between successive differential stages causes a progressive rise in DC bias voltage levels, shifting operating points, limiting output swing, and risking signal distortion.
To restore the DC output level to zero or a desired reference voltage, level translator circuits—typically emitter followers with resistive dividers—are inserted between stages.
Simple voltage-divider emitter followers can be enhanced with diode-based or current-mirror biasing for better thermal stability, lower output impedance, and more precise DC level shifting.

Differential Amplifier

Using superposition, when one input is grounded, the circuit behaves as an inverting or non-inverting amplifier, depending on which input is active.

In practical op-amps, resistor mismatch causes a small common-mode output, defined using Vcm = (Vi1 + Vi2)/2.
The quality of a differential amplifier is measured by CMRR

Precision Rectifier
Ordinary Si diodes have a cut-in voltage of 0.7 V which impedes efficient rectification
The circuit consists of an op-amp with a diode connected in the feedback path. The op-amp has a very high open-loop gain.
Because of this large gain, even a very small difference between the input and output causes the op-amp output to swing sufficiently to overcome the diode’s forward voltage. This effectively cancels the 0.7 V drop.
When the input voltage becomes positive, the op-amp output also becomes positive due to amplification even if input is 7 uV considering gain of 105

Schmitt trigger
A false triggering can happen in simple comparator due to input noise owing to high open loop gain of Op-amp.
To avoid that a positive feedback is provided from the output called regenerative comparator.
The positive feedback creates infinite closed loop gain if feedback gain is kept to unity.


Positive feedback creates two different switching levels. It remembers previous state.
If V0=+Vsat then output flips only Vi>V+. This voltage is called upper threshold voltage.

Waveform Generators
Operational amplifiers are commonly used to generate different types of waveforms such as sine, square, and triangular waves, which are required in electronic circuits.
Many analog and digital systems need periodic signals for purposes like timing, control operations, testing, and synchronization.
A sine wave is one of the most important and commonly used waveforms in electronics but generating an ideal sine wave with stable amplitude and frequency is comparatively difficult.
An oscillator is essentially a feedback amplifier. A portion of output is fed back to input to cause oscillations. 
The condition for oscillations should be unity feedback gain with 0- or 360-degrees phase angle.

RC-phase shift oscillator
LC oscillators require large inductors at low frequencies hence RC networks are used for oscillator design.
Two commonly used RC oscillators are the RC phase-shift oscillator and the Wien bridge oscillator.
In an RC phase-shift oscillator, the op-amp is operated in inverting configuration, producing a phase shift of 180 degrees.
The feedback RC network provides another 180 degrees phase shift totaling a 360 degrees phase shift between input and feedback signal.
The feedback network determines the oscillation frequency, while the amplifier provides the required gain to compensate for losses occurring in the RC sections.

Pulse Width Modulator
By giving modulating control signal at pin5 555 timer can act as a pulse width modulator.
A sequence of trigger pulses applied to pin 2 produces a corresponding sequence of output pulses at the output terminal.
The width of each output pulse depends on when the upper comparator resets the flip-flop, which occurs when the capacitor voltage reaches the comparator threshold.
The modulating signal applied at pin 5 alters the threshold voltage of the upper comparator. Normally this threshold is 2/3𝑉𝐶𝐶	​.
The modulating signal is superimposed on the internal voltage divider voltage, causing the threshold level to increase or decrease.
Because the capacitor must charge to this varying threshold, the time taken to reach the threshold changes, which changes the pulse width of the output.
The trigger pulses determine the output frequency, so the frequency remains constant (trigger pulse frequency), while the duty cycle varies according to the modulating signal.

Schmitt Trigger using 555 timer
The timer IC 555 can be used to function as a Schmitt trigger with variable threshold voltage levels.
The two internal comparator inputs (pins 2 and 6) are connected together and externally biased with a voltage VCC /2 through R1 and R2 potential divider network.
Since the voltage at pins 6 and 2 will trigger the upper comparator (UC) at (2/3)VCC and the lower comparator (LC) at (1/3)VCC , the bias provided by R1 and R2 is centered within these two threshold levels. 
When a sine wave input of sufficient amplitude (vi ), where vi is greater than [(2/3)VCC – (1/3)VCC] is applied to the circuit, it causes the internal flip-flop to alternatively Set and Reset generating a square wave output.

Phase Locked Loop (PLL)
With the advancement of integrated-circuit technology PLL circuits became fundamental building blocks in modern electronics
PLLs are widely used in systems such as FM stereo decoders, motor speed control systems, tracking filters, frequency synthesizers in transmitters and receivers, FM demodulators, FSK decoders, and local oscillator generation in TV and FM tuners.
A phase-locked loop consists of three main components:(1) Phase detector,(2) Low-pass filter, and(3) Voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO).
The phase detector compares the input frequency 𝑓𝑖​ with the feedback frequency from the VCO and produces an output proportional to their phase difference.
The output of the phase detector is a dc voltage proportional to the phase error, commonly called the error voltage.
The low-pass filter removes high-frequency noise and ripple from the detector output and produces a smoother dc control voltage.
In addition to filtering noise, the low-pass filter also determines the dynamic response and stability of the PLL.

Phase Locked Loop (PLL)
The filtered dc voltage is applied to the voltage-controlled oscillator, whose output frequency is directly proportional to the input control voltage.
The VCO output is continuously compared with the input signal, and its frequency is automatically adjusted until both signals match.
Before the input signal is applied, the VCO oscillates at its natural free-running frequency called free running state.
When the input signal is applied, the VCO frequency begins to change under the influence of the control voltage, and the PLL enters the capture mode.
When the VCO frequency becomes equal to the input frequency, the loop reaches the phase-locked condition.
Once locked, the PLL can track small changes in the input frequency by continuously adjusting the VCO frequency.

Phase detector
The PLL tries to force a Voltage Controlled Oscillator (VCO) to follow the phase and frequency of an input signal.
If two periodic signals are compared: 
If their phases are identical → error = 0
If one leads or lags → error ≠ 0
 The phase detector converts this phase difference into a voltage called the error voltage.
  
This voltage then passes through a loop filter and adjusts the VCO frequency until the phase difference becomes zero (or a fixed value depending on detector type).

Digital Phase detector
Digital phase detectors require square wave inputs.
They compare the timing of edges rather than waveform amplitude.
Three types are commonly used.
EXOR Phase detector
SR Flip flop phase detector
 The XOR phase detector uses an EXOR gate for the phase detection
The XOR gates goes high when both the signals are different.


So as long as the two signals have different edge timings, the XOR gate goes high generating a pulse signal.
The higher the phase difference the greater is the pulse width.
Based on pulse width the error voltage is generated and supplied to the VCO for frequency tuning.
The error voltage is highest for 180 degrees and lowest for 0 degree phase difference.
However, it works well only if both the signals are perfect square waves with 50% duty cycle.
